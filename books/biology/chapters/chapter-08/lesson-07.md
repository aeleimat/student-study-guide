## Concepts

1. **An enzyme's activity is affected by general factors (temperature, pH) and by chemicals that act on it specifically** (p45). Stated.
2. **Each enzyme has an optimal temperature and an optimal pH; optimal conditions favour its most active shape** (p46). Stated.
3. **Typical human enzyme optimum 37 degrees C; enzyme of thermophilic (heat-tolerant) bacteria 77 degrees C** (p47a). Drawn as two bell curves; each falls steeply past its peak.
4. **Pepsin (stomach) optimum pH about 2; trypsin (intestine) about 8** (p47b). Drawn as two bell curves.
5. **Cofactors are nonprotein enzyme helpers; inorganic (a metal in ionic form) or organic; an organic cofactor is a coenzyme; coenzymes include vitamins** (p48). Stated.
6. **Competitive inhibitors bind the active site and compete with the substrate; overcome by raising the substrate concentration** (p49, p51b). Stated and drawn.
7. **Noncompetitive inhibitors bind another part of the enzyme, change its shape and make the active site less effective** (p50, p51c). Stated and drawn.
8. **Toxins and poisons are often irreversible inhibitors; sarin binds covalently to serine in the active site of acetylcholinesterase** (p50). Stated.
9. Added from Campbell, not on the slides: past the optimum the enzyme **denatures** (loses its shape); too cold only slows it. A noncompetitive inhibitor is not rescued by more substrate.

## Vocabulary

- **Optimal temperature / optimal pH**: the value at which the rate is highest.
- **Denaturation**: loss of the enzyme's active shape, usually from too much heat or an extreme pH.
- **Thermophilic**: heat-loving; lives in hot places.
- **Pepsin, trypsin**: a stomach and an intestinal protein-digesting enzyme.
- **Cofactor**: nonprotein helper. **Coenzyme**: an organic cofactor, often made from a vitamin.
- **Competitive / noncompetitive inhibitor**; **irreversible inhibitor**.
- **Sarin, acetylcholinesterase, serine**.

### Questions

- `q1` id `c-denature`, `choice`: "A human enzyme works well at 37 degrees C but almost stops at 60 degrees C. Why?" Right: the heat unravelled it, so it lost its active shape (denatured). Wrong: the substrate ran out (substrate was not mentioned, the enzyme itself changed); its optimum moves up to 77 degrees C when heated (the optimum is a property of the enzyme and does not move); the cofactor melted into the substrate (not what the curve shows). Hint: look at what happens to the shape. Picture: the temperature curves with the human curve at 60 degrees C marked and the unfolded enzyme.
- `q2` id `c-inhib`, `grid` (competitive / noncompetitive, `multi: false`): rows: binds the active site (competitive); binds another part of the enzyme (noncompetitive); changes the enzyme's shape (noncompetitive); can be overcome by adding more substrate (competitive); looks like the substrate (competitive). Why: competitive inhibitors share the notch, noncompetitive ones act from elsewhere. Wrong hints: "noncompetitive" on the active-site row: it binds elsewhere; "competitive" on the shape-change row: that is the noncompetitive effect. Picture: panels (b) and (c) lit per row.
- `final1` ("Chapter practice"):
  - `p-opt` `blanks`, three rows: "human enzyme optimum" = 37 degrees C, "thermophilic bacteria optimum" = 77 degrees C; "pepsin optimum pH" = 2, "trypsin optimum pH" = 8; "an organic cofactor" = coenzyme, "an inorganic cofactor" = metal ion. Hint: read the peaks. Picture: both sets of curves with "?" tags at the peaks.
  - `p-hot` `choice`: "A hot-spring bacterium's enzyme is tested at 37 degrees C. What do you expect?" Right: slow, because it is well below its optimum of 77 degrees C. Wrong: it works fastest, because 37 degrees C is the best temperature for all life (the optimum depends on the enzyme); it is denatured at once (denaturation happens above the optimum, not below); it speeds up only if pH is changed (temperature already limits it). Picture: the two temperature curves with a line at 37 degrees C.
