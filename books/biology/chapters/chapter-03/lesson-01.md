## Concepts

In slide order. Hardness: definition only / explained / quantified.

1. **Polar covalent bonds make water a polar molecule** (p2, p3). Water's bonds are polar covalent, so its opposite ends carry opposite charges. p3 says the charged regions of a water molecule come from its polar covalent bonds. Hardness: explained (named as the cause; the bond-sharing mechanism is not described).
2. **Partial charges on water** (p2, p3). The H atoms carry a partial positive charge (δ+) and the O atom carries a partial negative charge (δ−). The slides use the δ notation only on p3. Hardness: explained (shown in figure; not defined as a term on the slides).
3. **Oxygen has two regions of partial negative charge** (p3). The slide attributes this to oxygen's electron arrangement. The arrangement itself is not described. Hardness: stated (the claim is given; the mechanism is not).
4. **Opposite partial charges attract, forming hydrogen bonds** (p2, p3). Regions of neighboring water molecules with opposite partial charges are attracted to each other; this attraction is a hydrogen bond. Hardness: explained (the rule is stated; the dotted-line figure shows it).
5. **Each water molecule can hydrogen-bond to several others** (p3). The slide gives no number. Hardness: stated.
6. **Hydrogen-bond associations are constantly changing** (p3). The slide says they change; it gives no reason or timescale. Hardness: stated, not explained.

p2 labels this material section 3.1 (the slide text reads "3.1. Polar covalent bonds in water molecules result in hydrogen bonding").

## Vocabulary

- **Polar molecule**: a molecule whose ends carry opposite charges (p2).
- **Polar covalent bond**: a covalent bond in which the shared electrons are pulled unevenly, so the atoms carry partial charges (p2, p3).
- **Partial charge (δ+, δ−)**: a charge smaller than a full ion's charge, marked δ+ (partly positive) or δ− (partly negative) (p3).
- **Hydrogen bond**: the attraction between opposite partial charges on neighboring molecules, here between water molecules (p2, p3).
- **Electron arrangement**: how electrons are distributed around an atom; the slide cites it as the reason oxygen has two regions of partial negative charge (p3).

## Errata

Decisions on the flagged problems (numbers refer to "Possible problems").

1. Hydrogen bonding is taught as caused by the polarity of the whole molecule (uneven sharing plus the bent shape), not by "the bonds" alone. The covalent bond is shown solid, the hydrogen bond dashed and between molecules.
2. The delta notation comes first, in `pull`, before anything is attracted. "Opposite ends have opposite charges" is said as partial charges: far smaller than an ion's charge, and no electron has left.
3. Electron arrangement: the lesson says oxygen has two pairs of electrons not used in bonds, giving two negative regions. The term "lone pair" is not used; no orbitals.
4. Electronegativity is named and taught in one beat (`share`): every atom pulls on shared electrons, oxygen pulls much harder than hydrogen. No scale or numbers.
5. Hydrogen bond type is stated: an attraction between molecules, not a covalent bond (no shared electrons). "Noncovalent" is not shown as a term.
6. Strength: "far weaker than the covalent bonds inside the molecule", no number (outside figures such as one twentieth are left out). The reason they change is the molecules' constant motion (jostling), from outside the slides but standard. Lesson 2 says "a fraction of a second"; this lesson says "a tiny fraction of a second", which agrees.
7. The space-filling figure is not used. Molecules are ball-and-stick and schematic; no distances or scale are read off (small "not to scale" tag).
8. "Several" is quantified: up to four (two from oxygen's negative regions, two from the hydrogens). Outside fact, standard; shown as "up to 4".
9. The section label "3.1" is not shown. The title card says chapter 3, lesson 1.

Outside facts used (for the coordinator): bond angle about 105 degrees (104.5 rounded); up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule; hydrogen bonds far weaker than covalent bonds (no number); jostling as the reason bonds keep changing. Terms not introduced: lone pair, dipole, tetrahedral, noncovalent.

## Plan

Dropped: figure 3.2's space-filling model, the section label 3.1, orbitals and lone-pair vocabulary, bond energies and lifetimes, other polar and nonpolar molecules.

Order: intro, part A (sharing, partial charges, shape, check 1), part B (attraction and the hydrogen bond, weak and changing, up to four, check 2), wrap, practice (4 questions in one set), finish. The slides' order is kept, with the background (bond, electronegativity) put first.

### Questions

- `q1` id `c-shape`, choice, "A straight molecule H-O-H with the same two polar bonds would be ..." Options: (a) just as polar, because oxygen still pulls harder; (b) not polar as a whole, because the two pulls point opposite ways and cancel (right); (c) positive all over; (d) polar only at the oxygen. Why: the two pulls are equal and opposite, so neither side is more positive; the bend is what puts both hydrogens on one side. Wrong (a): each bond is polar but in a line the pulls add up to nothing; (c): no electron has left, the charges are only partial and balanced; (d): polarity belongs to the whole molecule, a positive side and a negative side.
- `q2` id `c-hbond`, pickEls (click the picture), "Click the hydrogen bond." The picture: a central molecule and neighbors with four lines labelled A to D. A is a solid O-H inside the central molecule, B is a dashed line from the central molecule's H to a neighbor's O (right), C is a solid O-H inside a neighbor, D is a solid O-H inside another neighbor. Why: a hydrogen bond is the dashed link between molecules. Wrong on a solid line: that is a covalent bond inside a molecule, made of shared electrons.
- `final1` ('Practice'):
  - `p-charge` grid, delta+ / delta-, one answer per row (`multi: false`): the oxygen atom in water (delta-); a hydrogen atom in water (delta+); the part of a molecule that a neighbor's hydrogen is attracted to (delta-); the part of a molecule that points toward a neighbor's oxygen (delta+).
  - `p-count` blanks, a picture of a central molecule bonded to three neighbors with one position open: "The central molecule has ___ hydrogen bonds drawn." Answer 3 (counted from the drawn bonds). "At most ___ more could form." Answer 1 (4 minus the count, computed in the page's code).
  - `p-change` choice: "Hydrogen bonds in liquid water keep breaking and re-forming. Why?" Right: the molecules keep moving and jostling, and each bond is weak. Wrong: the molecules themselves keep breaking apart (they stay whole); hydrogen bonds are stronger than covalent bonds (they are weaker); oxygen gives its electrons to a neighbor (no electron leaves).
  - `p-true` grid, true/false: a hydrogen bond is a link between two water molecules (T); it is made of shared electrons like the O-H bonds (F, it is an attraction between partial charges); one water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds (T); in liquid water a given hydrogen bond lasts a long time (F, they keep breaking and re-forming); water is polar because its molecule is bent (T).
