## Concepts

In slide order. Hardness: definition only / explained / quantified.

1. **Water dissociates by proton transfer** (p25, p26). A hydrogen atom in a hydrogen bond between two water molecules moves from one to the other as a proton (H+). The molecule that gains it is a hydronium ion (H3O+); the one that loses it is a hydroxide ion (OH-). Hardness: explained (named steps; the figure shows it).
2. **Dissociation is rare but has large effects** (p27). Simplified as H2O into H+ and OH-. The slide says it is statistically rare but changes in H+ and OH- concentrations can drastically change a cell's chemistry. No frequency is given. Hardness: explained (qualitative).
3. **In pure water [H+] equals [OH-]** (p28). Equal concentrations define the neutral reference point. Hardness: definition plus a stated equality; the value is not given on the slide (see Numbers).
4. **Solutes called acids and bases change [H+] and [OH-]** (p28). Acids and bases alter the balance between H+ and OH-. Hardness: definition only.
5. **Acid and base, two definitions** (p29, p30). p29 defines an acid as a substance that increases [H+] and a base as one that reduces [H+]. p30 defines an acid as a proton donor and a base as a proton acceptor. The two framings are both on the slides; they are not reconciled. Hardness: defined (both forms).
6. **pH is the negative log of [H+]** (p30). pH = -log[H+]. Hardness: quantified (formula given; no worked example on the slides).
7. **Acidity rises as pH falls** (p30). Increasing [H+] raises acidity and lowers pH. Hardness: explained (one-line rule).
8. **The pH scale** (p31, p32). Acidic solutions are below pH 7, basic above 7. The pH of a solution depends on its relative hydrogen ion concentration. Most biological fluids lie between pH 6 and 8. The p32 figure places example solutions along the scale (see Figures and Numbers). Hardness: explained; the scale is shown, not computed.
9. **Cells keep internal pH near 7** (p33). The internal pH of most living cells must stay close to pH 7. Hardness: stated (no mechanism on the slide beyond buffers).
10. **Buffers resist pH change** (p33, p34). A buffer minimizes changes in H+ and OH- concentrations. Most buffers are an acid-base pair that combines reversibly with H+. Hardness: explained (definition plus composition).
11. **Buffer composition** (p34). A buffer is a weak acid plus its conjugate base, or a weak base plus its conjugate acid. Two examples: acetic acid/acetate (CH3COOH / CH3COO-) and ammonia/ammonium (NH3 / NH4+). Hardness: explained (equations given).
12. **Ka and pKa of weak acids** (p35). Ka is the dissociation constant of HA + H2O to H+ and A-. pKa = -log Ka. A smaller Ka means less dissociation, a larger pKa, and a weaker acid. Hardness: quantified (definition and direction; no numerical example on p35).
13. **Strong acids have no pKa** (p35). The slide concludes that only weak acids have pKa values. Hardness: stated (see Possible problems).
14. **Monoprotic and polyprotic acids** (p35). A monoprotic acid has one proton to lose (HCl, CH3COOH). A polyprotic acid has more than one (H2SO4, H3PO4). A polyprotic acid has one pKa per proton. Hardness: definition plus examples.
15. **Buffering range is pKa ± 1** (p36). A buffer resists pH change only within pKa ± 1. Hardness: quantified (rule of thumb given as a fixed range).
16. **Maximum buffering capacity at pH = pKa** (p36). Worked case: acetic acid, pKa 4.8, range 3.8 to 5.8, maximum capacity at pH 4.8. Hardness: quantified (worked example on the slide).
17. **Acid precipitation** (p37). Rain, snow or fog with pH below 5.6 is acid precipitation. It forms mainly when pollutants mix with water in the air. It damages life in lakes and streams and contributes to forest decline through soil chemistry. Hardness: definition plus effects; the chemistry of the pollutants is not given.

## Vocabulary

- **Hydrogen ion (H+) / proton**: a hydrogen atom with its electron left behind; the slide uses both names.
- **Hydronium ion (H3O+)**: a water molecule that has gained a proton. The slide also writes [H+] as [H3O+].
- **Hydroxide ion (OH-)**: a water molecule that has lost a proton.
- **Dissociation**: separation of a molecule into ions (here water into H+ and OH-).
- **Acid**: a substance that increases [H+] (p29); a proton donor (p30).
- **Base**: a substance that reduces [H+] (p29); a proton acceptor (p30).
- **pH**: a measure of [H+] in a solution; pH = -log[H+].
- **Neutral**: a solution where [H+] = [OH-].
- **Acidic / basic (alkaline)**: pH below 7 / above 7.
- **Buffer**: a solution that resists change in pH.
- **Conjugate base / conjugate acid**: the partner left after a weak acid gives up a proton / the partner formed when a weak base takes one.
- **Weak acid / weak base**: an acid or base that does not fully dissociate (the slides require this for pKa).
- **Ka**: dissociation constant of a weak acid.
- **pKa**: -log Ka; indicates extent of dissociation.
- **Monoprotic acid**: an acid with one proton to lose.
- **Polyprotic acid**: an acid with more than one proton to lose; one pKa per proton.
- **Buffering range**: pKa ± 1.
- **Buffering capacity**: how much acid or base a buffer can absorb; greatest at pH = pKa.
- **Acid precipitation**: rain, snow or fog with pH below 5.6.

## Possible problems

Flagged, not decided.

1. **p30 heading "Aids & Bases"** (typo for "Acids & Bases"). No content effect.
2. **p34 "Buffer: is a solution that can resist change in PH."** Capital "PH" is a typo for "pH".
3. **p34 base buffer equation "A + H2O <-> AH+ + OH-"** omits the charge on A (should be A-, or B for a generic base) and the "conjugate" label is not shown in the equation. Also the acid equation on p34 and p35 write H+ where the hydronium form H3O+ appears on p25 and p30. Decide whether the lesson uses H+ or H3O+ throughout.
4. **p35 "Thus, only weak acids have pKa, strong acids have no pKa."** Strong acids dissociate almost fully, so an equilibrium Ka is not defined in the usual way, but "no pKa" is stated without that reason. The lesson should give the reason or drop the strong-acid claim.
5. **p35 Ka/pKa direction.** "↓Ka → ↓dissociation → ↑pKa → the weaker the acid" is correct, but the line "and vice versa" is compressed. Stronger acid = larger Ka = smaller pKa. Students may invert it; the lesson should state the direction in one sentence.
6. **p35 "pKa indicates the extent of dissociation."** Loose; pKa indicates the strength of an acid (its tendency to dissociate), not the amount dissociated in a given solution.
7. **Two definitions of acid and base (p29 vs p30).** p29 is concentration-based (H+ increase), p30 is proton-transfer-based (proton donor). Both are on the slides and not reconciled. The lesson must decide whether to teach one framework or both.
8. **p25 "The hydrogen atom leaves its electron behind and is transferred as a proton."** Misleading: the hydrogen is transferred as a proton (nucleus) and the electron stays in the first molecule's bonding. Standard wording: a proton moves from one water molecule to another.
9. **p26 figure has no caption and no label for the dotted hydrogen bond.** The dotted line is read from context (text on p25).
10. **p27 "statistically rare" with no number.** The slide gives no frequency; the lesson should not give one from memory unless sourced.
11. **p28 "basic (the opposite of acidic)".** The parenthetical is vague; "basic" and "alkaline" are both used in the book and the slides use only "basic".
12. **p31 "Most biological fluids have pH values in the range of 6 to 8."** Fine as stated; note that the p32 figure places some fluids outside this range (gastric juice at 2, urine at 6, seawater at 8).
13. **p32 figure "rainwater" placement.** Placed between 5 and 6 on the scale; the exact value is not given. Do not quote a precise pH for rain from this figure.
14. **p32 vs p37 rain thresholds.** Rainwater appears on the p32 scale as an acidic example, but p37 defines acid precipitation as pH below 5.6. Ordinary rain is slightly acidic (dissolved carbon dioxide) and sits near 5.6. The slides do not explain the 5.6 threshold; the lesson should say where it comes from or note that it is a conventional cut-off.
15. **p36 "pKa ± 1" buffering range.** The slide states it as a fixed rule; in practice it is an approximate working range. Teach as a rule of thumb.
16. **p36 example mixes "pKa of acetic acid buffer = 4.8" wording.** The phrase "buffer = 4.8" should read "pKa of acetic acid = 4.8". Meaning is clear.
17. **p33 "Buffers" slide shows no figure or worked example** beyond the three bullets; the buffering concept rests on p34 and p36 alone.
18. **p35 monoprotic examples.** HCl is listed as a monoprotic acid with a pKa. HCl is a strong acid, so the same claim on p35 (strong acids have no pKa) conflicts with the example list. This needs deciding together with item 4.
19. **p37 "acid precipitation ... caused mainly by the mixing of different pollutants with water in the air."** The pollutants are not named on the slide. The lesson should either name them from its own knowledge (flagged as added) or keep the wording general.
20. **p38 blank.** The rendered page has no text and no figure. Check the source PDF page before treating it as part of the lesson.

### Questions

Numeric values are computed in the page's code.

- `q1` id `c-buffer`, choice: "A buffer holds acetic acid and acetate. Some H+ arrives. Which partner takes it up?" Options: (a) acetic acid, CH3COOH; (b) acetate, CH3COO- (right); (c) water only; (d) neither, the pH jumps. Why: acetate is the conjugate base; it takes up H+ and becomes acetic acid. Wrong (a): acetic acid is the partner that gives H+; (c) the pair does the job; (d) that is what a buffer prevents.
- `q2` id `c-strength`, choice: "Acid X has Ka = 1 x 10^-3 and acid Y has Ka = 1 x 10^-6. Which is the weaker acid?" Options: (a) X, pKa 3; (b) Y, pKa 6 (right); (c) Y, pKa 3; (d) the same strength. Why: smaller Ka, larger pKa, weaker acid. Wrong: hint on the direction.

  - `p-range` blanks: "A weak acid has pKa 6.8. Its buffering range runs from pH ___ to pH ___, and it buffers best at pH ___." 5.8, 7.8, 6.8 (pKa - 1, pKa + 1, pKa). Hint: pKa plus or minus one.
  - `p-conv` blanks: "Ka = 1 x 10^-4 gives pKa = ___" 4; "pKa = 7 gives Ka = 1 x 10^-___" 7; "the stronger acid has pKa 3 or pKa 6: ___" 3. Hint: stronger acid, larger Ka, smaller pKa.
