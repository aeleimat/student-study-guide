## Concepts

In slide order. Hardness: definition only / explained / quantified.

1. **Ice floats because its hydrogen bonds are more ordered** (p13, p14). Ice is less dense than liquid water. In ice the hydrogen bonds are stable and hold molecules in an open lattice; in liquid water they constantly break and re-form. Hardness: explained (the mechanism is named on p14; the lattice is only shown in the figure).
2. **Water is densest at 4 °C** (p13). Stated as a fact; the slide does not say why the density peaks there. Hardness: stated, quantified only as a temperature.
3. **If ice sank, bodies of water would freeze solid** (p13). The slide states the consequence ("making life impossible on Earth") and gives no mechanism. Hardness: stated (consequence).
4. **Solution, solvent, solute** (p15). A solution is a homogeneous mixture; the solvent is the dissolving agent; the solute is what is dissolved. Hardness: definition only.
5. **Water is a versatile solvent because of its polarity** (p15). Stated; the link to hydrogen bonds comes on p16. Hardness: stated (not explained on p15).
6. **Aqueous solution** (p15). A solution in which water is the solvent. Hardness: definition only.
7. **Water dissolves substances by forming hydrogen bonds** (p16). The slide says water is an effective solvent because it readily forms hydrogen bonds. Hardness: explained (named mechanism).
8. **Hydration shell around each ion** (p16, p17). When an ionic compound dissolves, each ion is surrounded by a sphere of water molecules. Hardness: explained; the p17 figure shows a Cl- ion and an Na+ ion (drawn as a green and a yellow sphere) in water, with the ionic crystal on the right.
9. **Nonionic polar molecules dissolve in water** (p18). Water also dissolves compounds made of nonionic polar molecules. Hardness: stated.
10. **Large polar molecules dissolve if they have ionic and polar regions** (p18). Proteins are the example; a protein dissolves if it has such regions. Hardness: stated (the condition is given, the mechanism is not).
11. **Hydrophilic and hydrophobic substances** (p19). Hydrophilic: has an affinity for water. Hydrophobic: does not have an affinity for water. Hardness: definition only (no examples on the slide).
12. **Solute concentration matters for reactions** (p20). Most biochemical reactions occur in water. Reactions depend on collisions between molecules and therefore on solute concentration in the aqueous solution. Hardness: stated (the collision link is asserted).
13. **Molecular mass** (p21). The sum of the masses of all atoms in a molecule. Hardness: definition plus rule (summing).
14. **Mole and Avogadro's number** (p21, p22). One mole is 6.02 × 10^23 molecules (p21) or 6.022 × 10^23 units of any substance, such as atoms, molecules or ions (p22); the slide names this number Avogadro's number. Hardness: definition plus value.
15. **Molar mass and dalton** (p22). Molar mass is the mass of one mole, in g/mol. The dalton (Da) is 1/12 of the mass of a 12C atom, and numerically equal to g/mol. Hardness: definition plus equivalence (stated, not derived).
16. **Number of moles = mass / molar mass** (p22). Hardness: quantified (formula).
17. **Molarity = moles of solute / litres of solution** (p21, p22). Expressed as M or mol/L. Hardness: quantified (formula).
18. **Worked example: molarity of NaCl** (p23). 0.3 kg NaCl in 700 mL water, with molar mass of NaCl = 58.44 g/mol. Answer on slide: 7.33 M. Hardness: quantified (see Numbers).

## Vocabulary

- **Solution**: a homogeneous mixture of substances.
- **Solvent**: the dissolving agent of a solution (water in the lesson).
- **Solute**: the substance that is dissolved.
- **Aqueous solution**: a solution in which water is the solvent.
- **Polarity**: uneven distribution of charge in a molecule, which lets water dissolve polar and ionic substances (used on p15 without definition).
- **Hydration shell**: the sphere of water molecules around a dissolved ion.
- **Hydrophilic**: having an affinity for water.
- **Hydrophobic**: not having an affinity for water.
- **Molecular mass**: sum of the masses of all atoms in a molecule.
- **Mole**: the amount that contains 6.02 × 10^23 (6.022 × 10^23 on p22) units of a substance.
- **Avogadro's number**: 6.022 × 10^23 (the number of units in one mole).
- **Molar mass**: mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol.
- **Dalton (Da)**: 1/12 of the mass of a 12C atom; numerically equal to g/mol.
- **Molarity (M)**: moles of solute per litre of solution.
- **Density**: mass per unit volume (used on p13 without a definition).

## Numbers

Stated on the slides:
- Water is at greatest density at 4 °C (p13)
- Molar mass of NaCl = 58.44 g/mol (p23)
- 1 mole = 6.02 × 10^23 molecules (p21); 6.022 × 10^23 units (p22)
- Dalton = 1/12 of the mass of a 12C atom (p22)
- Worked example: 0.3 kg NaCl = 300 g; 700 mL = 0.7 L (p23)
- Answer on p23: 7.33 M

Checked (recomputed):
- Molar mass of NaCl = Na 22.99 + Cl 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol. The slide's value is correct. (Atomic masses are not given on the slides.)
- Moles in 300 g NaCl = 300 / 58.44 = 5.133 mol.
- Molarity = 5.133 mol / 0.7 L = 7.334 M. **The slide's answer, 7.33 M, is correct** to the number of digits shown (7.3335 rounds to 7.33). The only issue is the volume used (see Possible problems).
- Check example for concept 17: 117 g NaCl in 2 L is 117 / 58.44 = 2.002 mol, so 1.00 M. Use this as a worked check for students (not on the slide).
- Avogadro's number on p21 and p22 differ only in precision (6.02 vs 6.022).

Not given on the slides: atomic masses of Na and Cl, the density of water at any temperature, and any value for the density of ice.

## Possible problems

Flagged, not decided.

1. **p23 "dissolved in 700 ml water" versus solution volume.** The slide divides by 0.7 L, the volume of water added. Molarity on p22 is defined on the volume of the solution. The solute adds volume, so the true solution volume is larger than 0.7 L and the molarity is slightly lower than 7.33 M. The lesson must choose: use "700 mL of solution" as the premise, or keep "700 mL of water" and say it is an approximation.
2. **p13 density claim has no reason.** "Water reaches its greatest density at 4 °C" is correct but unexplained; the ice-order reason on the same slide does not cover it. Students may think it follows from ice being less dense.
3. **p13 "making life impossible on Earth" is an unqualified consequence.** Correct as a summary of the freezing argument, but it is stated, not shown.
4. **p15 "polarity" is used without definition.** The slide calls water a versatile solvent "due to its polarity" and does not say what polarity is; the reason (uneven charge, hydrogen bonds) comes on p16.
5. **p16 "readily forms hydrogen bonds" is the whole mechanism.** It is stated for water in general; the slide does not show how the bonds pull ions apart.
6. **p17 hydration shell figure is schematic.** Ion and water sizes are not to scale. Also, the handwritten H+ label on p17 points at a water hydrogen, not a free proton; this is a student mark, not source, but students may copy it.
7. **p19 hydrophobic has no example.** The definition ("does not have an affinity for water") is given without a substance; oil or lipid is the expected example and is not on the slide.
8. **p20 "Most biochemical reactions occur in water" is asserted.** No source or example. The collision link is stated, not explained.
9. **p21 and p22 Avogadro's number precision.** 6.02 × 10^23 (p21) versus 6.022 × 10^23 (p22). Use one value in the lesson.
10. **p22 dalton wording.** "Expressed in g/mol or Dalton" mixes units. Dalton is a mass unit for single atoms or molecules (1/12 of a 12C atom); g/mol is per mole. They are equal in number, not in meaning. The lesson should say "numerically equal".
11. **p23 molar mass of NaCl is given without its atomic masses.** Students cannot check 58.44 from the slide alone. Decide whether to add Na and Cl atomic masses.
12. **p23 rounding.** 7.3335 is shown as 7.33 M. Fine; the lesson should say "about 7.33 M" if it keeps 0.3 kg and 0.7 L.
13. **p24 bond-type figure is not linked to the text.** Hydrogen bond appears as one of four bond types, but the slides never define it on that page. Decide whether the lesson defines hydrogen bond here or refers back to lesson 2.
14. **p13 ice-density handwritten note.** The margin note "ice density < liquid water density" is a student mark; it matches the slide text and is not a separate source claim.



## Errata

Decisions on the flagged problems (numbers refer to "Possible problems").

1. The slide's NaCl example (0.3 kg in 700 mL water, 7.33 M) is not used. Its arithmetic is right but it is physically impossible: NaCl dissolves to only about 36 g per 100 mL of water at room temperature, so 300 g will not dissolve in 700 mL. The lesson says the solubility limit once (about 36 g per 100 mL, "about") and uses realistic examples: 11.7 g NaCl made up to 250 mL = 0.2 mol / 0.25 L = 0.8 M; 58.5 g made up to 1 L = 1 M.
2. Molarity is always "per litre of solution". The lesson says "made up to ... of solution": dissolve, then add water to the mark of a flask. It states that the solute takes up volume, so the volume of water added is not the divisor.
3. "Greatest density at 4 degC" is stated and given a reason: just above freezing the water begins to lock into the open lattice, so it expands and stays near the top. This is outside-the-slide reasoning, kept to one sentence.
4. "Making life impossible on Earth" is not used. Shown instead: if ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up and could freeze solid; the floating ice lid slows heat loss (lesson 2 heat idea, only referred to) so the water below stays liquid.
5. Polarity is defined where it is first used: slightly negative oxygen end, slightly positive hydrogen ends (met in lesson 2, restated in one line).
6. "Readily forms hydrogen bonds" is shown as a mechanism: oxygen ends face a positive ion, hydrogen ends face a negative ion, pulling it out of the crystal.
7. The hydration-shell figure is drawn schematically with a "not to scale" tag (Na+ small, Cl- larger, as on the slide). The handwritten H+ label is not used; water hydrogens are labelled delta+.
8. Hydrophobic gets an outside example: oil (no charged or polar regions), with water bonding to itself and squeezing the oil into drops. Sugar is used as the nonionic polar example (outside, common).
9. "Most reactions happen in water" is kept as stated; the collision link is shown as a picture (more solute per litre, more collisions), not proved.
10. One value of Avogadro's number: 6.02 x 10^23. Moles are of "units" (atoms, molecules, ions; for salt, formula units).
11. Dalton: said as the mass of one molecule (water 18 daltons); molar mass is grams per mole (water 18 g/mol). "Same number, different units." Never "expressed in g/mol or Dalton".
12. Atomic masses are given in the lesson: H 1, O 16, Na 23, Cl 35.5 (outside, rounded). So H2O = 18 g/mol and NaCl = 58.5 g/mol. 58.44 is not used anywhere, so answers stay clean (117 g in 2 L = 1.0 M).
13. Rounding: none in the lesson; every example is exact with these masses.
14. Hydrogen bond is defined by reference to lesson 2 (not re-taught); the p24 bond-type tree is dropped.
15. Heat and temperature are not re-taught; the ice lid "slows the heat leaving", a one-clause link to lesson 2.
16. Colloids are not in the notes and are not taught.

### Questions

- `q1` id `c-icefloat`, choice, "Why does ice float on liquid water?" Options: (a) its molecules are heavier; (b) hydrogen bonds hold its molecules in an open lattice, further apart, so it is less dense (right); (c) its molecules stop moving, and cold things float; (d) ice is made of a different molecule. Why: the lock-in spacing lowers the density. Wrong (a): the same molecules weigh the same but take more room; (c): spacing, not stillness, is the reason; (d): it is still H2O.
- `q2` id `c-molar`, blanks, "29.25 g of NaCl (molar mass 58.5 g/mol) is made up to 0.5 L of solution. The molarity is ___ M." Answer 1 (29.25 / 58.5 = 0.5 mol; 0.5 / 0.5 = 1.0). Hint: first find moles, then divide by litres of solution. Compute in the page's code.
- `final1` ('Chapter practice'):
  - `p-moles` blanks: "Molar mass of water: ___ g/mol" 18; "36 g of water = ___ mol" 2.
