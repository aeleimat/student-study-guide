## Concepts

1. **Most cellular respiration needs O₂ to make ATP; glycolysis makes ATP with or without O₂** (p42). Stated.
2. **Without O₂, glycolysis couples with either fermentation or anaerobic respiration to make ATP** (p42). Stated.
3. **Anaerobic respiration uses an electron transport chain, but with an electron acceptor other than O₂, for example sulfate** (p43). Stated.
4. **Fermentation makes ATP by phosphorylation without an electron transport chain** (p43). Stated, with a loose printed word; see Possible problems.
5. **Fermentation is glycolysis plus reactions that regenerate NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep running** (p44). Stated.
6. **The two common fermentations are alcohol fermentation and lactic acid fermentation** (p44). Stated.
7. **Alcohol fermentation converts pyruvate to ethanol in two steps; the first step releases CO₂** (p45). Stated, drawn in panel (a) of slide 46.
8. **Yeast alcohol fermentation is used in brewing, winemaking and baking** (p45). Stated.
9. **Lactic acid fermentation reduces pyruvate, by NADH, to lactate, with no CO₂ released** (p47). Stated, drawn in panel (b) of slide 46. The printed wording is loose; see Possible problems.
10. **Lactic acid fermentation by some fungi and bacteria makes cheese and yogurt** (p47). Stated.
11. **Human muscle cells use lactic acid fermentation to make ATP when O₂ is scarce** (p47). Stated.
12. **Fermentation and aerobic respiration both start with glycolysis, but their final electron acceptors differ: an organic molecule in fermentation, O₂ in respiration** (p48). Stated.
13. **Respiration gives about 32 ATP per glucose (printed as "38 ATP or 32 ATP?"); fermentation gives 2 ATP per glucose** (p48). Stated; the printed figure is unsettled; see Possible problems.
14. **Obligate anaerobes do fermentation or anaerobic respiration and cannot survive in O₂** (p49). Stated.
15. **Yeast and many bacteria are facultative anaerobes: they can use either fermentation or cellular respiration** (p49). Stated.
16. **Pyruvate is a fork: with no O₂ it goes to fermentation products, with O₂ it goes to acetyl CoA and the citric acid cycle** (p49, p50). Stated and drawn on slide 50.

## Vocabulary

- **Fermentation**: glycolysis plus reactions that regenerate NAD⁺; no electron transport chain; ATP only by substrate-level phosphorylation.
- **Anaerobic respiration**: an electron transport chain whose final acceptor is not O₂ (sulfate, for example).
- **Alcohol fermentation**: pyruvate → acetaldehyde (CO₂ released) → ethanol; NADH gives its electrons to acetaldehyde.
- **Lactic acid fermentation**: pyruvate → lactate; NADH gives its electrons to pyruvate.
- **NAD⁺ regeneration**: turning NADH back into NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.
- **Final electron acceptor**: the molecule that takes the electrons at the end of the chain or pathway (O₂, sulfate, pyruvate, acetaldehyde).
- **Obligate anaerobe**: cannot survive in the presence of O₂.
- **Facultative anaerobe**: survives with or without O₂, by fermentation or respiration (yeast, many bacteria).
- **Pyruvate fork**: pyruvate's two routes, fermentation (no O₂) and aerobic respiration (O₂).
- **Substrate-level phosphorylation**: an enzyme moves a phosphate from a substrate to ADP to make ATP (the ATP route in fermentation).
- **Lactate, ethanol, acetaldehyde, acetyl CoA, citric acid cycle**: as the slides name them.

## Questions

No question slides in this chapter; the questions below come from the material and have answers.

1. Why does glycolysis stop making ATP in a cell with no NAD⁺ regeneration, and what does fermentation do about it? (Glycolysis needs NAD⁺ to accept electrons (2 NAD⁺ → 2 NADH). Without NAD⁺ it stalls. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ by passing NADH's electrons to an organic molecule (pyruvate or acetaldehyde), so glycolysis can continue.)
2. In alcohol fermentation, which step releases CO₂, and what is the final acceptor of the electrons? (The first step, pyruvate → acetaldehyde, releases CO₂. The final electron acceptor is acetaldehyde, which becomes ethanol.)
3. Lactic acid fermentation makes lactate from pyruvate. What is the NADH doing? (It gives its 2 electrons and an H⁺ to pyruvate, reducing it to lactate and regenerating NAD⁺.)
4. A yeast cell is grown with no O₂. Which ATP-making route does it use, and how much ATP per glucose does it make? (Fermentation: glycolysis plus NAD⁺ regeneration; 2 ATP per glucose.)
5. Why does a facultative anaerobe such as yeast make less ATP per glucose without O₂ than with O₂? (Fermentation has no electron transport chain and only substrate-level phosphorylation, so it makes 2 ATP per glucose; aerobic respiration with O₂ makes about 30 to 32.)
6. Name the three things that make an obligate anaerobe different from a facultative one. (Obligate: cannot survive in O₂ and uses only fermentation or anaerobic respiration. Facultative: survives with or without O₂ and can switch between fermentation and respiration. Both are catabolic routes from pyruvate; the difference is the O₂ tolerance and the switch.)
7. Why does a sulfate-using organism count as respiring, and not fermenting? (It runs an electron transport chain and makes most of its ATP by chemiosmosis, with sulfate as the final electron acceptor instead of O₂.)
8. Which of these is a fermentation product: ethanol, lactate, CO₂ from pyruvate oxidation, or acetyl CoA? (Ethanol and lactate. CO₂ from pyruvate oxidation is respiration (acetyl CoA step); acetyl CoA is the aerobic route into the citric acid cycle.)
