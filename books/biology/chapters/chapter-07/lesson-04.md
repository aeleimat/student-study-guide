## Concepts

1. **Tonicity is a solution's ability to make a cell gain or lose water** (p24). Hardness: stated.
2. **Isotonic: solute concentration equals the inside of the cell; no net water movement across the plasma membrane** (p24). Hardness: stated.
3. **Hypertonic: solute concentration greater than inside the cell; the cell loses water** (p24). Hardness: stated.
4. **Hypotonic: solute concentration less than inside the cell; the cell gains water** (p24). Hardness: stated.
5. **Hypertonic or hypotonic environments create osmotic problems for organisms** (p26). Hardness: stated.
6. **Osmoregulation is the control of solute concentration and water balance, and is an adaptation for life in such environments** (p26). Hardness: stated, defined.
7. **Paramecium is hypertonic to its pond water and has a contractile vacuole that acts as a pump** (p26; photo with the vacuole labelled). Hardness: stated; the slide does not say what the vacuole pumps or how it is powered.
8. **Cell walls help maintain water balance** (p27). Hardness: stated.
9. **A plant cell in a hypotonic solution swells until the wall opposes uptake; it is now turgid (firm)** (p27). Hardness: stated.
10. **A plant cell in an isotonic solution has no net water movement; it becomes flaccid (limp) and the plant may wilt** (p27). Hardness: stated.
11. **A plant cell in a hypertonic solution loses water; the membrane pulls away from the wall, called plasmolysis, usually lethal** (p28). Hardness: stated.
12. **Figure comparison: animal and plant cells in three solutions** (p25). An animal red blood cell goes lysed (hypotonic), normal (isotonic), shriveled (hypertonic); a plant cell goes turgid (hypotonic), flaccid (isotonic), plasmolyzed (hypertonic). The slide shows the outcomes; it does not name the water arrows in words. Hardness: shown by the figure; the lesson must read the figure for the arrows.

Note: concept 12 is the slide 25 figure, which carries most of the lesson's visual weight. The slides never use the word osmosis; it is taught in lesson 3.

## Vocabulary

- **Tonicity**: a solution's ability to cause a cell to gain or lose water.
- **Isotonic, hypertonic, hypotonic**: the three comparisons of a solution with the inside of the cell (solute concentration equal, greater, less).
- **Osmoregulation**: control of solute concentration and water balance.
- **Contractile vacuole**: the Paramecium organelle shown on p26.
- **Paramecium**: a protist in pond water.
- **Lysed**: a red blood cell that has burst (p25).
- **Normal** (animal, isotonic): the biconcave red blood cell in isotonic solution (p25).
- **Shriveled**: a red blood cell in hypertonic solution with spiky outline (p25). The slide does not name the process.
- **Turgid (firm)**: a plant cell pressed against its wall after taking up water (p27).
- **Flaccid (limp)**: a plant cell in isotonic solution; the plant may wilt (p27).
- **Plasmolysis, plasmolyzed**: the membrane pulls away from the wall in hypertonic solution; usually lethal (p28, p25).
- **Cell wall**, **plasma membrane**: the labels that show where the membrane detaches (p25).

## Questions

Question ideas this material supports:

1. A red blood cell is placed in a solution with more solute than its cytoplasm. Which way does water move, and what shape does the cell take? (Tests: hypertonic direction, shriveled outcome. Easy.)
2. A red blood cell bursts when placed in pure water. What is the solution type relative to the cell, and which way does water move? (Tests: hypotonic, lysis. Easy.)
3. Why does a plant cell in pure water become turgid but not burst? (Tests: the cell wall opposes uptake. Medium.)
4. A plant cell is in an isotonic solution and the plant wilts. What is the cell's state, and why is there no net water movement? (Tests: flaccid, isotonic. Medium.)
5. What is plasmolysis, and why is it usually lethal? (Tests: membrane pulls from the wall in hypertonic solution. Medium.)
6. Why does Paramecium need a contractile vacuole in pond water? (Tests: it is hypertonic to its surroundings, so water keeps entering. Medium.)
7. Define osmoregulation and say why it matters in a hypertonic or hypotonic environment. (Tests: definition and the adaptation. Easy.)
8. Rank a red blood cell, a plant cell and a Paramecium by how they cope with a hypotonic environment. (Tests: comparing the three cases from the figure and the photo. Hard.)
9. Put these in order from most to least water inside the plant cell: plasmolyzed, turgid, flaccid. (Tests: reading the figure's states. Easy.)
10. A plant cell and an animal cell are both moved from isotonic to hypotonic solution. Which one bursts, and why does the other not? (Tests: the wall's role; combines slides 25 and 27. Hard.)

## Possible problems

1. **Printed, loose (p26):** "The protist Paramecium, which is hypertonic to its pond water environment". Tonicity describes a solution relative to a cell. Strictly, the Paramecium's cytoplasm is hypertonic to the pond water, which makes the pond water hypotonic to the cell. Teach it as the cell's inside having more solute than the pond, not as a property of the organism.
2. **Printed, loose (p26):** "has a contractile vacuole that acts as a pump". The slide does not say what it pumps (water, out of the cell) or that it uses ATP. Teach it as expelling excess water, and flag that the word "pump" comes from the slide; the energy detail belongs to lesson 5.
3. **Printed, ambiguous (p25):** the word "normal" labels two different states: the animal cell in isotonic solution ("Normal") and the plant cell in hypotonic solution ("Turgid (normal)"). Teach the plant's "normal" as turgid, not isotonic, so the two uses do not collide on screen.
4. **Printed, missing text layer (p25):** the whole figure has no text layer; every label was read from the PNG. Nothing printed looks missing from the image, but the figure's labels must not be copied from the text file, which has none.
5. **Printed, spelling variant (p25 vs p28):** the figure says "Plasmolyzed" (US spelling) and the text says "plasmolysis". They name the same process. Use one spelling consistently in the narration.
6. **Figure could mislead (p25):** the red blood cell and the plant cell are not drawn to the same scale, and the plant cell's membrane gaps in the plasmolyzed case are drawn larger than life. Do not quote sizes from the figure.
7. **Figure does not name a process (p25):** "Shriveled" (the red blood cell in hypertonic solution) and "Lysed" are the only words for these states. The slide does not name the crenation or lysis process; the narration may name them, as a textbook fact.
8. **Handwritten, all marks checked (p25):** yellow highlighter over every label: Hypotonic, Isotonic, Hypertonic, Lysed, Normal, Shriveled, Cell wall, Plasma membrane (both plant cells), Turgid (normal), Flaccid, Plasmolyzed, and the H2O labels. Each highlighted label is correct for its panel, and each leader points to the right structure (the cell wall to the wall of the hypotonic plant cell; the membrane in the plasmolyzed cell to the membrane pulled away from the wall). The marks are emphasis only; no wrong claim is made. The red spell-check line under "Plasmolyzed" is a software mark, not a claim. Nothing handwritten on p24, p26, p27 or p28.

### Questions

- `q1` id `c-tonic`, `grid` (columns: water in / water out / no net movement, `multi: false`): "Which way does the water go?" Rows: a cell in a solution with more solute than the cell (out; hypertonic); less solute (in; hypotonic); the same solute (no net movement; isotonic). Why: water moves toward more solute; equal means equal rates both ways. Wrong hints: "in" for hypertonic, the outside has more solute so water leaves; "no net movement" for hypotonic, the concentrations differ.
- `q2` id `c-vacuole`, `choice`: "What does the contractile vacuole do for Paramecium?" Right: pumps out the water that keeps entering. Wrong: takes up water so the cell grows (the pond is hypotonic to the cell, so water already floods in); builds a cell wall (Paramecium has none); digests food (that is a food vacuole with lysosomes). Picture: the vacuole squirting.
- `final1` ("Chapter practice", 6 questions):
  - `p-rbc` `choice`: "A red blood cell is placed in a hypertonic solution. What happens?" Right: it loses water and shrivels. Wrong: it swells and lyses (that is hypotonic); no change (that is isotonic); it becomes turgid (a plant-cell word, and it needs a wall). Picture: the shriveled cell with arrows out.
