## Concepts

1. **Membrane potential is a voltage across the membrane.** (Slide 36.) Stated as a definition. Easy: the slide gives it in one sentence. Teach the cause next: the voltage comes from uneven distribution of positive and negative ions on the two sides.
2. **Two forces together make the electrochemical gradient.** (Slide 37.) A chemical force (the ion's concentration gradient) and an electrical force (the effect of the membrane potential on the ion). Medium: the slide names both forces in a lettered list but does not show how they combine or which way they point for a given ion.
3. **Electrogenic pumps generate voltage.** (Slide 38.) An electrogenic pump is a transport protein that makes voltage across the membrane. The sodium-potassium pump is the major one in animal cells; the main one in plants, fungi and bacteria is a proton pump. Easy as a list; the slide gives no mechanism.
4. **Pumping charged ions out builds the potential, and the pump stores energy.** (Slide 39, figure.) The proton pump moves H⁺ from cytoplasm to extracellular fluid, with ATP supplying the energy; the figure shows + signs outside and − signs inside. The slide does not say that charge separation is the point, so the lesson must state it. Hard: the slide has no text; the idea comes from the picture. Slide 38 says pumps "help store energy that can be used for cellular work", which this figure illustrates.
5. **Cotransport: active transport of one solute indirectly drives transport of another.** (Slide 40.) Plants use the H⁺ gradient made by proton pumps to drive active transport of nutrients into the cell. Medium: "indirectly" is the key idea, and the slide gives no example until the next slide.
6. **The sucrose-H⁺ cotransporter couples two solutes.** (Slide 41, figure.) The H⁺ that the proton pump made flows back in through the cotransporter, and this downhill flow carries sucrose into the cell against its own concentration gradient. Hard: two red arrows of different kinds (solid and dotted) and two sucrose positions must be read together; the slide gives no text explaining the direction of each arrow.

Chapter 7 ideas used here: "electrochemical gradient" connects Chapter 7's diffusion lessons to ion movement; the sodium-potassium pump is named but not drawn in this lesson (it belongs to lesson 5's cycle).

## Vocabulary

- membrane potential: the voltage difference across a membrane (slide 36)
- electrochemical gradient: the combined chemical and electrical forces that drive an ion across a membrane (slide 37)
- chemical force: the ion's concentration gradient (slide 37)
- electrical force: the effect of the membrane potential on an ion's movement (slide 37)
- electrogenic pump: a transport protein that generates voltage across a membrane (slide 38)
- sodium-potassium pump: the major electrogenic pump of animal cells (slide 38)
- proton pump: the main electrogenic pump of plants, fungi and bacteria (slides 38, 39, 41)
- cotransport: active transport of one solute indirectly drives transport of another (slide 40)
- cotransporter: a membrane protein that carries two solutes together; here the sucrose-H⁺ cotransporter (slide 41)
- ATP: the energy source the proton pump uses (slides 39, 41)
- cytoplasm, extracellular fluid: the two sides of the membrane in the figures (slides 39, 41)

## Questions

No question slides; the narration step writes checks and practice.

Question ideas (each tests what it names):
1. Which side of the membrane is negative in a cell with a membrane potential, and why do the signs matter for an ion's movement? (Membrane potential sign convention, slide 36 and figures.)
2. Name the two forces that make up the electrochemical gradient, and say which one a positive ion feels when it is inside a negative cell. (Chemical and electrical forces, slide 37.)
3. Why is the proton pump called electrogenic? (Pumping charged ions out creates voltage, slides 38 and 39.)
4. Which organisms use proton pumps as their main electrogenic pump, and which pump does animal cells use? (Slide 38.)
5. In the proton pump figure, what energy source drives H⁺ out of the cell? (ATP, slide 39.)
6. What does "cotransport" mean, in one sentence, and why is it "indirect"? (Slide 40.)
7. In the sucrose-H⁺ cotransporter, which direction does H⁺ move, and what does that movement do for sucrose? (Slide 41.)
8. Sucrose moves into the plant cell against its concentration gradient. What supplies the energy, directly and indirectly? (Proton pump ATP gives H⁺ gradient; cotransport uses it, slides 40 and 41.)
9. Explain why the proton pump builds stored energy that can be used for cellular work. (Slide 38, last bullet.)
10. A drug blocks the proton pump. Predict what happens to sucrose uptake through the cotransporter. (Application: cotransport depends on the H⁺ gradient, slide 40.)

## Errata

1. Membrane potential size: the slides give no number. The voice says "from about minus fifty to minus two hundred millivolts" for animal cells, sourced to the textbook (the brief), never to the slides. The voltmeter in the picture reads about minus seventy, tagged "typical".
2. Slide 36 "voltage is created by differences in the distribution of ions": taught with the textbook detail the slide omits: the extra charge is a thin layer on the two faces of the membrane and the bulk fluid stays neutral. Picture: + and - signs only along the membrane faces.
3. Slide 37 "electrochemical gradient drives the diffusion of ions": the slide never says the two forces can agree or disagree. The lesson shows both: sodium (both point in) and potassium (chemical out, electrical in; the stronger force wins, no numbers). Chloride is used in the practice (chemical in, electrical out).
4. Slide 38 "pumps help store energy": said as a gradient, "like water behind a dam"; the energy is stored in the gradient plus voltage, not in the pump. The sodium-potassium pump is not redrawn; the voice only recalls "three sodium out, two potassium in" from lesson 5 (brief, textbook).
5. Slide 39 (no text layer, read from the PNG): H+ goes cytoplasm to extracellular fluid, ATP drives it, signs - inside and + outside. Nothing wrong. The slide never says charge separation is the point; the lesson says it outright (beat `proton`, mark `charge`).
6. Slide 41 dotted red arrow: it is H+ diffusing back in, not a pump step. The voice says "the dotted arrow on the slide, diffusion of hydrogen ions"; in the picture it is a dashed cream arrow "down the gradient", and the orange arrow with the ATP spark is the pump ("against").
7. Slide 41 sucrose "against its gradient": not stated on the slide; it is the textbook point and the voice says it ("even when there is already more sucrose inside"). The picture shows more sucrose dots inside than outside.
8. Slide 41 handwriting: loops round ATP, the inside H+, the cotransporter and the blue arrow above the pump are all right (energy source, the proton pumped, the cotransporter, H+ moving out); none is used as source. The scribble in the pump is unreadable and ignored.
9. "Indirectly": spelled out as "the cell never spends ATP on sucrose itself; the pump spent it on protons". Practice asks it.
10. Voice: H+ is "hydrogen ion" or "proton", never "H plus"; ATP is written `{{ATP|A T P}}`; mV is "millivolts". Slide 41 draws sucrose as a green hexagon plus pentagon and H+ grey; the book's colours apply (sucrose two linked gold hexagons, H+ pink).
11. Slide 38 has no title: the lesson title stands in. Slide 36's "How Ion Pumps Maintain Membrane Potential" is a section heading only.
