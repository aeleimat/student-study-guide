## Concepts

1. **Denaturation: the destruction of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure; the chain unfolds and is irregularly arranged ("denaturation means unfolding")** (p20). Stated; figure on p20. Easy; the lesson runs the strip backwards from lesson 3.
2. **The primary structure and the peptide bonds stay intact in denaturation** (not on the slides as a sentence, but implied by "destruction of secondary, tertiary and quaternary"). Drawn only (the chain keeps its sequence in the p20 figure). Medium: teach it explicitly, from the slide's list of levels destroyed.
3. **Causes of denaturation: high temperature, high pressure, vigorous shaking, strong acids and alkalis, UV light and irradiation, X-rays** (p21). Stated. Easy; the lesson can sort them into heat, mechanical, chemical and radiation groups for practice.
4. **Effects: loss of enzyme catalytic activity, loss of hormone function, decreased solubility, increased viscosity, increased digestibility (cooking meat and eggs by boiling)** (p21). Stated. Easy to medium; the cooking example links to chapter 2's egg albumin.
5. **Misfolding: proteins that cannot form a stable 3D structure are recognized as misfolded; they form large, insoluble aggregates and lose structure and function** (p22). Stated. Medium: the difference between denatured (unfolded) and misfolded (wrong fold that aggregates) is the lesson's key idea.
6. **Alzheimer's disease: caused by deposition of misfolded aggregates of amyloid β protein, forming dense fibrous plaques in the brain and nervous system, leading to neurodegeneration** (p22). Stated. Medium: the slide says "caused by"; see Possible problems.
7. **Symptoms: memory loss, dementia and lack of recognition** (p22). Stated. Easy.
8. **Clinical case: a 72-year-old man with two years of worsening recent memory, failing to recognize relatives, losing everyday function; diagnosis Alzheimer's disease; cause amyloid β misfolding** (p23). Stated as a case card with two questions. Medium: the case is the lesson's application; the learner solves it.

## Vocabulary

- **Denaturation**: destruction of the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, with the chain unfolded; primary structure kept (p20).
- **Causes of denaturation**: heat, pressure, shaking, acids and alkalis, UV light, X-rays (p21).
- **Catalytic activity**: the enzyme's ability to speed a reaction; lost on denaturation (p21).
- **Solubility and viscosity**: falls and rises on denaturation (p21).
- **Digestibility**: how easily enzymes break the protein down; rises on denaturation (p21).
- **Misfolded protein**: a protein that cannot form a stable 3D structure (p22).
- **Aggregate**: a large, insoluble clump of misfolded protein (p22).
- **Amyloid β protein**: the protein whose misfolded aggregates form the plaques of Alzheimer's disease (p22, p23).
- **Dense fibrous plaques**: the deposits in the brain (p22).
- **Neurodegeneration**: loss of nerve cells and their function (p22).
- **Dementia**: the decline of memory and thinking (p22).

## Possible problems

- **Slide 22 "Alzheimer's disease: Caused by deposition of misfolded aggregates of amyloid β protein"**: overstated. The amyloid β plaques are a hallmark, and the amyloid cascade is a leading hypothesis; the cause of most (sporadic) Alzheimer's disease is not settled. Standard texts (Lippincott) say the plaques are associated with the disease. Flag; do not decide here.
- **Slide 22 and 23 (amyloid β only)**: incomplete. Standard texts also name tau protein neurofibrillary tangles as a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease. The slides do not mention tau. Flag for the decision in Errata.
- **Slide 22 "dense fibrous plaques" in the brain and nervous system**: plaques form mainly in the brain; the nervous-system wording is loose but not wrong. Teach "in the brain".
- **Slide 23 case photograph**: a real person's photograph with an Arabic caption (a meme-like still, from a film). The brief says no humans; the case card should use a brain outline, not this picture. The lesson should not use this image or name the person. Flag for the coordinator.
- **Slide 23 "sever memory impairment"**: typo for "severe". Read as "severe" in the voice; not on screen.
- **Slide 21 "Decreased solubility" and "Increased viscosity"**: typical for denatured proteins that aggregate; the lesson should say these are typical effects, not every protein.
- **Slide 24**: stock photo with a motivational quote; not content, not used.

## Errata

- **"Caused by deposition of misfolded amyloid β" (slide 22)**: teach the lecture's answer (the case's cause is "misfolding of amyloid β protein"), but voice it as "the hallmark of the disease and the leading explanation for its cause" and the case question as "possible cause" (slide 23 itself says "possible"). Screen: "amyloid β plaques", with the word "possible" on the cause card.
- **Tau**: one short clause in `alz` ("a second protein, tau, also tangles inside nerve cells, but our slides focus on amyloid beta"), drawn as a small grey tangle inside one neurone; never asked in a question.
- **"Brain and nervous system" (slide 22)**: teach "in the brain"; plaques drawn only on the brain outline and its close-up.
- **Slide 23 photograph and slide 24 stock photo**: not used. A text case card plus a flat brain outline instead.
- **"sever memory impairment"**: read and written "severe".
- **Solubility down, viscosity up (slide 21)**: taught as the typical case (voiced in `effects`: "typical case, not for every protein"); the practice grid asks only the typical direction.
- **Peptide bonds stay (not a slide sentence)**: taught explicitly from "destruction of secondary, tertiary and quaternary"; the screen keeps the orange peptide bonds lit while the weak bonds (cyan H-bonds, salt bridges) vanish. Disulfide bridges: not claimed either way ("the weak bonds that held the fold").
- **Everyday examples for the causes**: whisking egg white (shaking) and acid curdling milk are added by us, voiced as examples only.

## Questions

In-lesson `q1` and `q2` are the slide's own two case questions (answers as printed on slide 23, checked against standard texts; "possible cause" kept).

- `q1` id `d-diag`, `choice`: "What is the diagnosis?" Right: Alzheimer's disease. Wrong: sickle-cell disease (one changed amino acid in the primary structure; no memory loss); a fever (heat would have to denature much of the body's protein); scurvy (a vitamin shortage). Hint: memory loss, dementia, no recognition. Picture: the case card.
- `q2` id `d-cause`, `choice`: "What is the possible cause?" Right: misfolding of amyloid β protein, forming plaques. Wrong: heat denaturing brain proteins (heat is not the cause); hydrolysis of peptide bonds (misfolding keeps the bonds); too many essential amino acids (not a diet disease). Hint: a protein that cannot form a stable shape clumps. Picture: the brain with plaques.
- `final1` ("Chapter practice"):
  - `p-keep` `choice`: "Which structure survives denaturation?" Right: the primary structure (sequence and peptide bonds). Wrong: the α-helices and β-sheets (secondary, destroyed); the folded globule (tertiary, destroyed); the subunit packing (quaternary, destroyed). Picture: the strip with all but the bead chain dimmed.
  - `p-sort` `grid` (columns Physical / Chemical): "Which kind of cause?" Rows: whisking egg white (physical, why "shaking"); curdling milk with acid (chemical, "acid"); a pressure cooker (physical, "pressure"); strong alkali on egg (chemical, "alkali"). Picture: the six cause tiles dimmed except the row's.
  - `p-effect` `grid` (columns Falls / Rises): "What happens on denaturation?" Rows: solubility (falls, "clumps"); viscosity (rises, "thickens"); digestibility (rises, "chain open"); enzyme activity (falls, "shape lost"). Picture: the effect rows.
  - `p-egg` `choice`: "Why is a boiled egg easier to digest than a raw one?" Right: heat unfolds the protein so enzymes reach its peptide bonds. Wrong: heat breaks every peptide bond already; heat adds amino acids to the protein; heat folds the protein into a tighter shape. Picture: the egg with an unfolded chain and the enzyme chips.
  - `p-vs` `choice`: "How does a misfolded protein differ from a denatured one?" Right: it never reached a stable fold, and it clumps into insoluble aggregates. Wrong: it has broken peptide bonds; it has a different amino acid sequence by definition; there is no difference. Picture: the two mini rows from `misfold`.
  - `p-levels` `blanks` (numbers): "Of the four levels of structure, denaturation destroys ___ and keeps ___." Answers 3, 1. Hint: only the primary survives. Picture: the strip with the counts hidden (page-local word or number blanks as in Biology lesson 2 if needed).
