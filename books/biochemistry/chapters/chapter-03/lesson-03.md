## Concepts

1. **Tertiary structure: the three-dimensional shape of one chain** (p15). Stated. Easy.
2. **Secondary structures (α and β) are arranged into the final functional 3D structure, called a domain** (p15). Stated. Medium: "domain" is used here for the whole tertiary structure; standard texts use domain for a compact unit within a tertiary structure (see Possible problems).
3. **Function depends on tertiary structure; disrupting it loses activity** (p15). Stated. Easy; ties to lesson 4's denaturation.
4. **Bonds that stabilize tertiary structure come from side chains R: disulfide bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic forces, electrostatic (ionic) forces** (p15 list; p16 drawing). Stated and drawn. Medium: the four are the same forces as lesson 1, now between side chains rather than backbone atoms.
5. **Two broad classes by 3D shape: globular and fibrous** (p17). Stated (the table is the content). Medium: the axial-ratio test is the new idea.
6. **Globular proteins: axial ratio below 10 (about 3 to 4), compactly folded and coiled chains; examples insulin, plasma albumins and globulins, enzymes** (p17). Stated and drawn. Easy.
7. **Fibrous proteins: axial ratio above 10, chains coiled in a spiral or helix and cross-linked by hydrogen bonds; examples keratin, myosin, collagen, fibrin** (p17). Stated and drawn. Easy.
8. **Quaternary structure: the association of several subunits into a closely packed arrangement; each subunit has its own primary, secondary and tertiary structure** (p18). Stated. Medium: "subunit" is the new term.
9. **Quaternary bonds: hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between non-polar side chains** (p18). Stated. Medium: the slide's list is short (see Possible problems).
10. **Examples: hemoglobin and lactate dehydrogenase are tetramers (4 subunits); creatine kinase is a dimer (2 subunits)** (p18, pictures). Stated and drawn. Medium: the hemoglobin picture labels α and β chains and heme with Fe²⁺.
11. **The four levels together, as one picture: primary sequence, secondary (helix and sheet), tertiary (folded globule), quaternary (complex of molecules)** (p19). Drawn; summary. Easy; this is the lesson's recap, and it is the strip the chapter's thread uses.

## Vocabulary

- **Tertiary structure**: the 3D shape of a single chain (p15).
- **Domain**: a region of the folded chain with its own fold, as the slide uses the word (p15). See Possible problems.
- **Globular protein**: compact, roughly spherical, axial ratio under 10 (p17).
- **Fibrous protein**: long, rod-like or coiled, axial ratio over 10 (p17).
- **Axial ratio**: length divided by width of the molecule (p17). The slide uses it without defining it; define it in the lesson.
- **Subunit**: one chain inside a multi-chain complex (p18).
- **Quaternary structure**: the arrangement of subunits (p18).
- **Tetramer, dimer**: complexes of four and two subunits (p18).
- **Hemoglobin (Hb), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK)**: the examples (p18).
- **Heme and Fe²⁺**: the iron-containing group in hemoglobin's picture (p18).

## Possible problems

- **Slide 15 "Formed of: Secondary structures (α and β) are arranged to form final functional 3D structure of protein called domain"**: wrong or loose. In standard biochemistry the whole folded chain is the tertiary structure; a domain is a compact region within it (Lippincott, Vasudevan). The slide's sentence calls the tertiary structure a domain. Flag; do not decide here.
- **Slide 16 tertiary figure, "Iconic bond"**: misspelt for "ionic bond" on the slide. Teach "ionic bond" (salt bridge). The drawing shows NH₃⁺ and an O⁻ on a carboxylate-like group; the side-chain drawing is loose (the group drawn is a ketone-like C=O next to CH₂, not a clear carboxylate).
- **Slide 16 "Hydrophobic interaction" box**: the branched CH with four methyl groups is a leucine- or valine-type side chain; the slide does not name the residue. Fine as a picture, but do not name it from the drawing.
- **Slide 17 table, "Fibrous proteins ... cross-linked by hydrogen bonds"**: loose. Fibrous proteins are often cross-linked by covalent bonds too (for example the disulfide cross-links of keratin, and collagen's cross-links). Teach the hydrogen-bond part as the slide's wording and note the covalent cross-links only if asked.
- **Slide 17 "Examples include: keratin, myosin, collagen and fibrin"**: fibrin is often classed as a fibrous protein, but in some texts it is placed with the plasma proteins. Accept the slide, note if the source disagrees.
- **Slide 17 "Globular ... axial ratio < 10 (from 3-4)"**: the "from 3-4" is unclear (it means the ratio is about 3 to 4). Teach as "about 3 to 4".
- **Slide 18 "Bonds (Forces): ... 1. Hydrogen bonds 2. Hydrophobic interactions (van der Waals forces) between non-polar side chains"**: van der Waals is again used as a name for hydrophobic interactions (the brief's rule: not the same thing). The quaternary list is also short: standard texts also name electrostatic (ionic) interactions and sometimes disulfide bonds as stabilizing subunit contacts. Flag the omission; do not add it without deciding.
- **Slide 18 "Hb (Hb) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) enzyme ... are tetramers"**: correct (LDH is a tetramer). **"Creatine kinase (CK) ... is dimer"**: correct (CK is a dimer of two subunits).
