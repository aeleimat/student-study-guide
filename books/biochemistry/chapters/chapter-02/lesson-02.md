## Concepts

1. **Proteins fall into three classes: simple (formed only of amino acids), conjugated or compound (protein plus a non-protein part), and derived (products of hydrolysis of simple or compound proteins)** (slide 7 tree). Easy. Stated and drawn. Note the derived definition differs from slides 12 and 15 (see Possible problems).
2. **Simple proteins give only amino acids on hydrolysis** (slide 8 definition). Easy. Stated.
3. **Albumin (egg albumin, lactalbumin in milk, serum albumin in blood) and globulin (egg globulin, lactoglobulin in milk, serum globulin in blood)** (slide 8 table). Medium. Stated as examples with their sources.
4. **Histones are basic proteins rich in basic amino acids; they are present with nucleic acid, packing DNA into nucleosomes** (slide 8 table). Medium. Stated. Link back to chapter 1's basic amino acids (arginine, lysine).
5. **Albuminoids (scleroproteins) are keratin (hair, nail) and collagen (skin, connective tissue, tendon, bone); they are supportive and protective** (slide 8 table and the bottom-right cell). Medium. Stated.
6. **Heat coagulation: albumin and globulin are coagulated by heat. Coagulation is a change from a liquid to a solid or a thicker liquid** (slide 8 table, the spanning cell under Albumin and Globulin). Medium. Stated. Histone is in the "not coagulated by heat" column.
7. **Four properties of albuminoids ("4 NOT"): not coagulated by heat, not digested by enzymes, not soluble in water, not found in plants** (slide 8 bottom-right cell). Medium. Stated as a mnemonic list.
8. **Gelatin is made from collagen by boiling; it forms a gel on cooling, is easily digested, and has low biological value because it lacks tryptophan** (slide 9). Medium. Stated, with a jelly photo and a collagen-versus-gelatin panel.
9. **Biological value: high-value proteins contain all essential amino acids (casein of milk, albumin, globulin); low-value proteins lack one or more (gelatin, gliadin, vegetable proteins)** (slide 10). Medium. Stated. Ties to chapter 1's essential amino acids; teach it as the test of a protein's quality.

## Vocabulary

- **Simple protein**: a protein formed only of amino acids (slides 7-8).
- **Conjugated (compound) protein**: a protein with a non-protein part (slide 7; lesson 3).
- **Derived protein**: a product of hydrolysis of simple or compound proteins (slide 7; lesson 3 for denaturation products).
- **Albumin, globulin**: simple proteins coagulated by heat; egg, milk and blood examples (slide 8).
- **Lactalbumin, lactoglobulin**: the milk albumin and globulin (slide 8).
- **Histone**: a basic protein rich in basic amino acids, bound to DNA in nucleosomes (slide 8).
- **Albuminoid (scleroprotein)**: fibrous, supportive proteins; keratin and collagen (slide 8).
- **Keratin**: the protein of hair and nail (slide 8).
- **Collagen**: the protein of skin, connective tissue, tendon and bone (slide 8).
- **Coagulation**: a change in protein structure from a liquid to a solid or thicker liquid (slide 8).
- **Gelatin**: a protein made from collagen by boiling; forms a gel on cooling (slide 9).
- **Biological value**: how completely a protein supplies the essential amino acids (slide 10).
- **Essential amino acid**: one the body cannot make and must get from the diet (slide 10 uses the term; chapter 1 defines it).
- **Casein, gliadin**: milk protein (high value) and wheat protein (low value) (slide 10).

## Questions

No question slides. Self-checks the slides imply:

1. Which of the three classes does egg albumin belong to, and why? (Simple: it gives only amino acids.)
2. Which simple proteins are coagulated by heat? (Albumin and globulin.)
3. Name one albuminoid and where it is found. (Keratin in hair and nail; collagen in skin, connective tissue, tendon and bone.)
4. Why is gelatin a low-value protein? (It lacks tryptophan, an essential amino acid.)
5. Is casein a high- or low-value protein, and what does that mean? (High: it contains all essential amino acids.)

## Possible problems

- **Slide 9 against slide 8: gelatin's class.** Slide 8 files gelatin in the simple-protein table (marked "**"). Slide 9 says gelatin is obtained from collagen by boiling, which is a denaturation or hydrolysis product. By the slide's own definitions (slide 12, and slide 15's definition), a product of denaturation or hydrolysis is a derived protein. Standard texts (Lippincott, Vasudevan) class gelatin as derived. Flag for a decision in Errata.
- **Slide 7 against slides 12 and 15: derived proteins.** Slide 7 defines derived proteins only as "product of hydrolysis of simple or compound proteins". Slides 12 and 15 define them as denaturation or hydrolysis products. The slide 7 definition is incomplete. Flag.
- **Slide 8, "**" and "4 NOT" cell.** The asterisks in "Gelatin**" are not explained on any slide. In the albuminoid cell, "Supportive, protective function" and "Gelatin**" sit below the four NOT bullets, so it is unclear whether gelatin is meant to be an albuminoid. Flag with the gelatin point above.
- **Slide 8, "Types" line.** Protamine, glutelin and gliadin appear in the list of simple proteins but have no table row or example. Gliadin is used later (slide 10) as a low-value protein. Flag as a list without support.
- **Slide 9, "deficient in some AA as tryptophan"**: "as" should read "such as". Gelatin lacks tryptophan; the wording suggests that tryptophan is one of several deficiencies, which the slide does not show. Loose wording, not decided.
- **Slide 10, "vegetable proteins" as a low-value class**: a blanket statement. Many plant proteins are low in one or more essential amino acids, but soy protein is nutritionally complete. Flag as a loose generalization.
- **Slide 10, "casein of milk"**: casein is a phosphoprotein (a conjugated protein, lesson 3, where the slide calls it "caseinogen"). The same protein has two names and two classes across the lessons. Flag for consistent naming in the voice (teach "casein" and say it is a phosphoprotein in lesson 3).
- **Slide 8, histone in the "not coagulated" column**: consistent with the layout (the coagulated cell spans albumin and globulin only). Checked against layout, not a claim to decide.

## Errata

- **Gelatin's class**: teach the lecture's placement (gelatin sits with the simple proteins, slide 8, starred) and say aloud, in the `note` mark, that many texts file it under derived proteins because it is made from collagen by boiling. Screen: the gelatin tile sits on the simple branch with its two stars and a small amber tag "many texts: derived". The practice never asks which class gelatin is in.
- **Derived definition (slide 7)**: teach slide 7's wording (products of hydrolysis of simple or compound proteins) and add one spoken line that lesson three adds denaturation products. Nothing is drawn beyond the slide's tree.
- **The "**" and the 4 NOT cell**: the stars flag gelatin only. Gelatin is not an albuminoid: the screen keeps the four NOTs on keratin and collagen, and gelatin gets its own tile.
- **"Types" line**: albumin, globulin, histone and albuminoids are taught; protamine, glutelin and gliadin are named in one sentence as grey "named, not drilled" chips (no invented examples); gliadin returns as the wheat protein in biological value.
- **"deficient in some AA as tryptophan"**: voice and screen say plainly "lacks tryptophan". The slide's wording is not read out.
- **"Vegetable proteins" as low value**: teach the lecture's line (it is examined), then correct it in the voice: many plant proteins are short of an essential amino acid, soy is complete, and mixing (rice with beans) fills the gaps. Screen: a small "soy is complete" chip and a rice + beans pair. Practice uses only gelatin, gliadin and milk/egg examples.
- **Casein**: always "casein" in this lesson (slide 10). One spoken line says it is a phosphoprotein (lesson three, "caseinogen"). No second class is drawn here.
- **Albuminoids "not digested by enzymes"**: taught as the lecture's list, spoken as "not digested by the usual enzymes" (collagen is attacked by collagenase, so the point is kept soft). Gelatin's "easily digested" is contrasted with collagen.
- **Histone column**: consistent with the layout (not coagulated by heat). Positive charge against DNA's negative charges is added as a light aside.

## Questions

Checks (side cards):
- `q1` id `c-heat`, `choice`: "Which simple proteins are coagulated by heat?" Right: albumin and globulin. Wrong: histone and keratin (neither coagulates by heat); collagen and gelatin (collagen does not, gelatin gels on cooling). Hint: think of a frying egg. Picture: the two cards with the heat arrow.
- `q2` id `c-gel`, `choice`: "Why does gelatin have a low biological value?" Right: it lacks tryptophan, an essential amino acid. Wrong: it cannot be digested (it is easily digested); it is not made of amino acids (it is); it is coagulated by heat (it gels on cooling). Hint: the empty slot on the chain. Picture: the gelatin chain with the dashed tryptophan gap.

Practice `final1` ("Chapter practice"), 7 questions:
- `p-simple` `choice`: "Why is egg albumin a simple protein?" Right: hydrolysis gives only amino acids. Wrong: it gives amino acids and sugar (that is conjugated); it is a product of hydrolysis of another protein (that is derived); it is found in the egg (so is much else). Picture: the tree with a hydrolysis arrow to beads.
- `p-heat` `grid` (columns Coagulated / Not coagulated, `multi: false`): "Does heat coagulate it?" Rows: egg albumin (Coagulated), serum globulin (Coagulated), histone (Not), keratin (Not). Why lines: "albumin: yes", "globulin: yes", "histone: no", "keratin: no". Picture: an egg frying beside a nail.
- `p-histone` `choice`: "What do histones do, and why are they suited to it?" Right: they pack DNA in nucleosomes, being rich in basic (positive) amino acids that hold the negative DNA. Wrong: they carry oxygen in blood (hemoglobin); they strengthen hair and nail (keratin); they coagulate to form a clot (they do not coagulate by heat). Picture: the nucleosome spool.
- `p-albuminoid` `choice`: "Which statement fits the albuminoids, keratin and collagen?" Right: they are not coagulated by heat and not soluble in water. Wrong: they are coagulated by heat like albumin; they are found in plants as storage proteins (not in plants); they dissolve freely in water (not soluble). Picture: hair and tendon icons beside the crossed-out NOTs.
- `p-bv` `grid` (columns High / Low, `multi: false`): "Which biological value?" Rows: casein of milk (High), egg albumin (High), gelatin (Low), gliadin of wheat (Low). Why lines: "all essential", "all essential", "no tryptophan", "short of some". Picture: two shelves with gold chips.
- `p-lysine` `choice`: "A protein lacks lysine, an essential amino acid (chapter one). What is its biological value?" Right: low, because it lacks an essential amino acid. Wrong: high, because it has all the others; it depends only on how much is eaten; it becomes high when boiled. Picture: a chain with a dashed gold gap.
- `p-blanks` `blanks` (numbers): "The lecture sorts proteins into ___ classes; albuminoids have ___ NOT properties; biological value has ___ levels." Answers 3, 4, 2.
