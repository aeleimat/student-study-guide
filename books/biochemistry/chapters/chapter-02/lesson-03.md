## Concepts

1. **Conjugated (compound) proteins are proteins joined to a non-protein part; there are six types** (slide 11 heading "Types of conjugated proteins: (6)"). Easy. Stated.
2. **Glycoproteins have carbohydrate as the non-protein part; examples are hormones (FSH, LH), antibodies, blood-group antigens, receptors and enzymes** (slide 11, row 1). Medium. Stated. The function column is blank.
3. **Lipoproteins have lipid as the non-protein part; they are chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL and HDL, which transport lipid in the blood** (slide 11, row 2). Medium. Stated.
4. **Phosphoproteins have phosphoric acid as the non-protein part; caseinogen is the main protein of milk** (slide 11, row 3). Easy. Stated.
5. **Metalloproteins carry metals (Cu, Zn, Fe): ceruloplasmin (Cu) carries and stores copper, insulin (Zn) regulates blood glucose, transferrin carries iron, ferritin (Fe) stores iron** (slide 11, row 4, read across the a-d lettering). Medium. Stated. The slide gives the functions in the same a-d order as the examples.
6. **Chromoproteins carry a pigment: hemoproteins have heme (red pigment) and flavoproteins have flavin (yellow pigment); hemoglobin and myoglobin carry oxygen, cytochromes carry electrons, and FAD and FMN carry hydrogen (reducing equivalents)** (slide 11, row 5, read with the colour swatch). Medium to hard. Stated; the row is dense, so teach it in two beats (heme pieces, then the flavin pieces).
7. **Nucleoproteins have nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) as the non-protein part; chromosomes are histone plus DNA, and ribosomes are protein plus RNA** (slide 11, row 6). Medium. Stated. The function column is blank. Link back to lesson 2's histones.
8. **Derived proteins are the denatured or hydrolytic products of simple or conjugated proteins** (slide 12 definition). Easy. Stated.
9. **Primary derived proteins come from denaturation (denaturing agents such as heat): no peptide bond is broken and the molecular weight does not change; the examples are coagulated albumin and coagulated globulin** (slide 12, column 1; slide 13 left). Medium. Stated. The chain on slide 13 runs protein → protean → metaprotein (coagulated protein).
10. **Secondary derived proteins come from hydrolysis (hydrolysing agents such as enzymes): peptide bonds break and the molecular weight falls; the chain runs proteoses → peptones → peptides → amino acids** (slide 12, column 2; slide 13 right). Medium. Stated. The arrows on slide 13 are the teaching point: the chain shortens step by step.
11. **Contrast: primary keeps every peptide bond and only changes shape; secondary breaks peptide bonds and shrinks the molecule** (slides 12-13, rows 3 and the bracket labels). Medium. Stated as a comparison; this is the lesson's closing sort.

Not taught: slide 14 has no protein content (see Figures).

## Vocabulary

- **Conjugated (compound) protein**: a protein with a non-protein part (slide 11).
- **Non-protein part (prosthetic group)**: the carbohydrate, lipid, phosphate, metal, pigment or nucleic acid added to the protein (slide 11). The slide does not use the word "prosthetic".
- **Glycoprotein**: a protein with carbohydrate (slide 11).
- **Lipoprotein; chylomicron, VLDL, LDL, HDL**: lipid-carrying proteins and particles (slide 11).
- **Phosphoprotein; caseinogen**: a protein with phosphoric acid; the milk protein (slide 11).
- **Metalloprotein; ceruloplasmin, transferrin, ferritin, insulin**: proteins with a metal (slide 11).
- **Chromoprotein; hemoprotein, flavoprotein**: a protein with a coloured pigment (slide 11).
- **Heme**: the red iron-containing pigment (slide 11).
- **Flavin; FAD, FMN**: the yellow pigment and its nucleotide forms that carry hydrogen (slide 11).
- **Cytochrome**: a heme protein that carries electrons (slide 11).
- **Nucleoprotein**: a protein with nucleic acid, such as a chromosome or a ribosome (slide 11).
- **Derived protein**: a product of denaturation or hydrolysis (slide 12).
- **Primary derived protein (1°)**: a product of denaturation; no peptide bond broken (slides 12-13).
- **Secondary derived protein (2°)**: a product of hydrolysis; peptide bonds broken (slides 12-13).
- **Denaturing agent**: heat, for example (slide 12).
- **Hydrolysing agent**: enzymes, for example (slide 12).
- **Protean, metaprotein**: the first and second primary derivatives in the slide 13 chain (slide 13). The slide does not define them (see Possible problems).
- **Proteose, peptone, peptide**: the secondary derivatives in order (slides 12-13).
- **Molecular weight (MW)**: the mass of the molecule; it falls for secondary derivatives (slide 12).

## Questions

No question slides. Self-checks the slides imply:

1. Which conjugated type carries copper, zinc or iron, and what does each metal do? (Metalloproteins: ceruloplasmin carries copper, insulin is zinc-linked, transferrin carries iron, ferritin stores iron.)
2. What is the non-protein part of a glycoprotein, a lipoprotein and a nucleoprotein? (Carbohydrate, lipid, nucleic acid.)
3. Which pigment is heme, and which hemoprotein carries oxygen? (Heme is red; hemoglobin and myoglobin carry oxygen.)
4. What is the difference between primary and secondary derived proteins? (Primary: denaturation, peptide bonds intact, molecular weight unchanged. Secondary: hydrolysis, peptide bonds broken, molecular weight falls.)
5. Put these in order from largest to smallest: peptone, amino acid, proteose, peptide. (Proteose, peptone, peptide, amino acid.)

## Possible problems

- **Slide 11, function column blank for glycoproteins and nucleoproteins.** Rows 1 and 6 have examples but no function, while rows 2-5 have one. Incomplete; teach the examples without a function for these two rows, or add function from the textbooks in the voice.
- **Slide 11, insulin as a metalloprotein.** Insulin is listed under metalloproteins for its zinc. Standard texts usually call it a zinc-containing hormone. Flag whether to keep it in the metalloprotein row.
- **Slide 11, transferrin has no metal symbol.** Transferrin is the iron carrier, but the row prints only "Fe" for ferritin. The reader has to infer it. Flag as loose.
- **Slide 11, "Enzymes" under glycoproteins.** The row lists enzymes as glycoproteins without a qualifier. Only some enzymes are glycosylated. Flag as a loose generalization.
- **Slide 11, "Caseinogen" as the milk protein.** Lesson 2 calls the milk protein "casein". The printed name here is the precursor name, caseinogen. Flag for consistent naming.
- **Slide 13, the primary chain.** "Protean" is shown but never defined on slides 12-13, and "metaprotein" is equated with "coagulated protein" in brackets. The slide 12 examples are only coagulated albumin and coagulated globulin. Flag: the protean step and the coagulated-protein equation both need a check against standard texts before the voice uses them.
- **Slide 13, the figure labels "1ry" and "2ry"**: the printed boxes use "1ry" and "2ry" (the text layer read them as primary and secondary). The figure's own symbols are 1° and 2°. Use "primary" and "secondary" in the voice.
- **Slide 12 against slide 7 (lesson 2)**: slide 12 defines derived proteins as denatured or hydrolytic products; lesson 2's slide 7 says hydrolysis only. Repeated here so the decision is made once (see lesson 2).
- **Slide 14, quote photo**: a motivational stock quote with no source and no protein content, placed in a lecture. It is not a figure and should not be taught or cited. The brief's "slide 14 is a figure" note should be read as "a stock photo with no content".
- **Slide 12 and slide 13, the molecular weight claim for primary derivatives**: "no change in MW" is printed as fact. Denaturation does not break peptide bonds, so this is consistent; no problem found. Noted so the voice keeps it.
