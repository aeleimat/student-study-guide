# Biochemistry team brief (common to every worker)

Book: **Biochemistry** (`books/biochemistry/`, `content/biochemistry/`, `site/biochemistry/`), Papermorph **Lessons** tier, tier 2 (illustrated scenes). This is the repo's second book. Its look, voice and models are the **Biology** book's (`books/biology/`, `site/biology/`), whose lessons are the bar. Repo root: `d:\Apps\student-study-guide` (Windows; use `python` or `uv run`). Skill: `.claude/skills/papermorph/` (`SKILL` below), third-party, never edit it. Read `CLAUDE.md` at the repo root first. It explains the nested `chapter-NN/lesson-NN/` layout and the engine workarounds, which this book copies exactly with `biochemistry` in place of `biology`.

Sources: three medical-biochemistry lecture-slide PDFs ("Medical Biochemistry & Molecular Biology", after Vasudevan, Chatterjea and Lippincott), one per chapter, in `books/biochemistry/source/`. The slides are short and dense: bullet lists, tables and textbook figures. **There are no question slides** (only chapter 3 has a clinical case, slide 23). Every lesson writes its own quick checks and practice set (6–8 questions) from what its slides teach. Several lessons have only 2–4 content slides. Fill them to 6–8 minutes with worked examples, comparisons, "spot the class" sorting, everyday foods and practice, not padding.

| chapter-02/lesson-01 | What Proteins Are and What They Do | 1–6 | proteins are organic, complex nitrogenous compounds made of C, H, O, N (± S); polymers (chains) of amino acids = polypeptides; amino acids joined by the peptide link –CO–NH– (the COOH of the first joins the NH₂ of the second, water released); the chain's two ends, N-terminal (free NH₂) and C-terminal (free COOH); biological importance (slide 6): enzymes, protein hormones, hemoglobin, plasma proteins, lipoproteins, immunoglobulins (antibodies), supporting structures (cartilage, bone, skin, nail, hair) |
| chapter-02/lesson-02 | Simple Proteins and Biological Value | 7–10 | the three classes (slide 7): simple (amino acids only), conjugated or compound (protein + non-protein part), derived (products of hydrolysis or denaturation of the others); **simple proteins** (slide 8): albumin (egg albumin, lactalbumin, serum albumin) and globulin (egg globulin, lactoglobulin, serum globulin), both coagulated by heat (coagulation = liquid → solid or thicker); histones (basic proteins rich in basic amino acids, packing DNA in nucleosomes); albuminoids or scleroproteins (keratin in hair and nail; collagen in skin, connective tissue, tendon, bone), not coagulated; gelatin (slide 9) from collagen by boiling, forms a gel on cooling, easily digested, low biological value (lacks tryptophan); **biological value** (slide 10): high (all essential amino acids: milk casein, albumin, globulin) vs low (lacking one or more: gelatin, gliadin, vegetable proteins). Ties back to chapter 1's essential amino acids |
| chapter-02/lesson-03 | Conjugated and Derived Proteins | 11–15 | **conjugated proteins** (slide 11 table): glycoproteins (carbohydrate: hormones FSH and LH, antibodies, blood-group antigens, receptors, enzymes), lipoproteins (lipid: chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, HDL; lipid transport in blood), phosphoproteins (phosphoric acid: caseinogen, the main protein of milk), metalloproteins (metals: ceruloplasmin Cu, insulin Zn, transferrin and ferritin Fe: carry and store copper, blood glucose, iron transport, iron storage), chromoproteins (pigment: hemoglobin etc.; read the rest of the row on slide 11), any further rows the slide has (nucleoproteins?) — open the image; **derived proteins** (slides 12–13): primary derivatives (products of denaturation, by heat etc., no peptide bond broken, no change in molecular weight: coagulated albumin, coagulated globulin) vs secondary derivatives (products of hydrolysis, peptide bonds broken, molecular weight falls: proteoses → peptones → peptides → amino acids); slide 14 is a figure: open it |
| chapter-03/lesson-01 | Protein Folding and the Bonds That Hold It | 1–9 | protein folding: a polypeptide chain folds into its biologically active native 3D structure; structure decides function; the four levels named (primary → quaternary) as the chapter's map only (lessons 2–3 teach them); wide variation in sequence → different shapes; the bonds: **covalent (strong)**: peptide bond –CO–NH– (COOH + NH₂, water removed), disulfide bond (two cysteine SH groups lose 2 H → S–S bridge); **non-covalent (weak)**: hydrogen bonds (C=O of one peptide bond to N–H of another nearby), electrostatic interactions (ionic bonds, salt bridges between oppositely charged side chains), hydrophobic interactions between non-polar side chains (no true bond, but they matter: they gather inside, away from water) |
| chapter-03/lesson-02 | Primary and Secondary Structure | 10–14 | **primary**: number, type and sequence of amino acids; main bond the peptide bond; one changed amino acid can cause disease (a physiological defect: sickle-cell hemoglobin is a fair example if kept brief); N-terminal (free NH₂ of the first) and C-terminal (free COOH of the last); amino acids counted from the N-terminal (slide 11 numbered chain 1…20); **secondary**: the chain folded by hydrogen bonds between peptide-bond atoms into the **α-helix** (coiled, right-handed, 3.6 amino acids per turn, H-bonds within the same chain, intrachain: C=O of one peptide bond to the N–H four residues along) and the **β-sheet** (pleated or zigzag strands side by side, H-bonds between neighbouring strands, interchain or between segments of one chain; parallel and antiparallel if slide 14 shows them); slides 13 and 14 are figures |
| chapter-03/lesson-03 | Tertiary and Quaternary Structure | 15–19 | **tertiary**: the 3D shape of one chain; secondary structures (α and β) arranged into the final functional structure (domains); the final level for a single chain; function depends on it, and disrupting it loses activity; held by interactions between side chains R: disulfide bonds, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions (slide 15's list; read it all); slide 17: two broad classes by 3D shape, fibrous and globular (open the image); **quaternary**: several subunits packed together, each with its own primary, secondary and tertiary structure, held by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions; examples: hemoglobin and lactate dehydrogenase (tetramers, 4 subunits), creatine kinase (dimer, 2 subunits); slide 19 is a figure (likely a summary of all four levels) |
| chapter-03/lesson-04 | Denaturation and Misfolding | 20–25 | **denaturation**: destruction of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, the chain unfolds and is irregularly arranged ("denaturation means unfolding"); primary structure and peptide bonds stay; causes: high temperature, high pressure, vigorous shaking, strong acids and alkalis, UV light and irradiation, X-rays; effects: enzymes lose catalytic activity, hormones lose function, solubility falls, viscosity rises, digestibility rises (cooking meat and eggs); **misfolding**: proteins that cannot form a stable 3D structure aggregate (large, insoluble) and lose function; Alzheimer's disease: misfolded amyloid β aggregates form dense fibrous plaques in the brain → neurodegeneration (memory loss, dementia, failing recognition); the clinical case (slide 23: a 72-year-old with two years of worsening memory → diagnosis Alzheimer's disease, cause amyloid β misfolding) as a case card the learner solves; slide 24 is a figure |

**Check the slides.** Handwritten marks, if any, are a student's notes, not source, and some may be wrong. Check every circled or handwritten answer. Printed slide text can be loose too. Examples: ch3 slide 9 calls hydrophobic interactions "van der Waals" (they are not the same thing; teach hydrophobic interactions and mention van der Waals forces only as the weak attractions among the packed chains, if at all); ch3 slide 12 "Bleated" means pleated; "serin, Valin"; ch1's essential list counts arginine and also calls it semi-essential. Check claims against standard biochemistry (Lippincott, Vasudevan), list wrong or loose ones under `## Possible problems`, then decide them in `## Errata`. Teach the lecture's own lists and mnemonics (students are examined on them) and correct only what is wrong.
